## war-between-rival-claimants-anarchy-1135
- Mechanism: two rival claimants after a death; settled by years of war, church councils shifting recognition, and finally a treaty naming the rival as heir.
- Period and place: England and Normandy, 1135–1154.
- Who may contest: Stephen of Blois (Henry I's nephew, crowned with church support and London's acclaim in 1135) and Empress Matilda (Henry I's daughter, named his heir after the White Ship drowning of his only legitimate son in 1120), with her half-brother Robert of Gloucester and husband Geoffrey of Anjou.
- How it is settled: war from 1138 and Matilda's invasion in 1139; Stephen captured at Lincoln in February 1141; a church council under the papal legate (Stephen's brother Henry of Blois) reaffirmed Stephen in 1141; stalemate 1143–1146; Geoffrey took Normandy; Archbishop Theobald refused to crown Stephen's son Eustace without papal approval; the Church brokered a truce at Wallingford in 1153; the Treaty of Wallingford let Stephen keep the throne and named Matilda's son Henry as his successor; Eustace died during the talks.
- Observed rates or durations: about 15 years of war (1138 to November 1153); Stephen died in 1154 and Henry succeeded. No rate beyond this single case. The chronicler's own figure is "nineteen winters while Stephen was king" (same Avalon page), a length of reign rather than of fighting.
- When no one prevails: stalemate led to local peace agreements by barons from the late 1140s; the article says the civil war was effectively over by then apart from occasional fighting.
- Source: https://avalon.law.yale.edu/medieval/ang12.asp (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Peterborough text, entries for 1135–1154; primary; Londoners received Stephen and the archbishop crowned him at midwinter; the earls turned to the empress only after Stephen was captured; Archbishop Theobald and others mediated an agreement that Stephen keep the throne for life and Henry succeed, with the barons swearing to keep the peace; the chronicle places this in its 1140 entry, not 1153, which differs from the Wikipedia dating above)
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anarchy (sections Succession, Civil war, Final phases, Introduction; secondary; the fetched summary read about the first 100,000 characters)

## nephew-versus-brother-1199-and-treaty-recognition
- Mechanism: two kin of different degree claim after a death (a brother and a dead elder brother's son); settled by who is crowned and which lords support, then confirmed by treaty with the rival's patron.
- Period and place: Angevin lands (England, Normandy, Anjou, Brittany), 1199–1200.
- Who may contest: John (surviving son of Henry II, favoured by Norman law per the article) and Arthur I of Brittany (son of John's elder brother Geoffrey, favoured by Angevin law), with Philip II of France backing Arthur.
- How it is settled: Richard died on 6 April 1199; John was crowned at Westminster on 27 May 1199 with most English and Norman nobles and his mother Eleanor behind him; Arthur had most Breton, Maine and Anjou nobles; William des Roches switched from Arthur to John; the Treaty of Le Goulet (May 1200) had Philip recognise John as Richard's heir for the French lands, setting Arthur's wider claims aside temporarily, in return for John accepting Philip's feudal overlordship there.
- Observed rates or durations: about 14 months from death to treaty recognition. The English side of the precedent (the *casus Regis* and how long uncle-over-nephew lasted) is in SOURCES.md, english-descent-with-representation, Pollock & Maitland vol. II pp. 282–286. Read there: uncle-over-nephew lasted from 1199 until at least 1246 (jurors still declining to decide) and was gone by the Edwardian books; no rate of contests is given.
- When no one prevails: not stated in the source; the article says medieval law gave little clear guidance and Richard had begun treating John as heir presumptive late in his reign.
- Source: https://archive.org/details/historyofenglish0000fred_u0k3 (Pollock & Maitland, History of English Law vol. II, ch. VI "Inheritance" §2 "The Law of Descent", about pp. 282–286; primary-period scholarship; at Richard's death only two children of Henry II were alive; the nephew out of possession had no self-help and no action against an uncle in possession because of the *casus Regis*, "so long as that case endures" no judgment against the uncle; a case of about 1201 was adjourned sine die; jurors in 1246 refused to say whether uncle or nephew was heir; the *casus Regis* is gone from the Edwardian law books by p. 286, when the nephew can recover by writ of right; a footnote calls the nephew's exclusion by Bracton's account "an abuse introduced by John")
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John,_King_of_England (section Early reign 1199–1204, "Accession to the throne, 1199"; secondary; first ~100,000 characters read)

## arbitration-by-a-court-the-great-cause
- Mechanism: no heir of the royal line; the guardians of the realm invite an outside ruler to arbitrate, who sits a court of auditors drawn from both claimants and himself.
- Period and place: Scotland, 1290–1292.
- Who may contest: thirteen put themselves forward; four had genuine claims (John Balliol, Robert de Brus, John Hastings, Floris V of Holland), and only Balliol and Brus had realistic grounds.
- How it is settled: Queen Margaret died on 26 September 1290; the Guardians asked Edward I of England to arbitrate to avoid civil war; he first demanded recognition of his overlordship and temporary control of the main royal castles; a court of 104 auditors (24 chosen by Edward, 40 by each of Brus and Balliol); judgment for Balliol in November 1292 (17 November per the article) on primogeniture rather than nearness of blood; Brus's late proposal to divide the kingdom was rejected.
- Observed rates or durations: about two years from vacancy to judgment; the court was adjourned ten months while Floris searched for documents; Floris withdrew in summer 1292 and his second claim was rejected in November 1292. Balliol reigned 1292–1296.
- When no one prevails: not stated in the source (the article describes only Balliol's selection).
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Cause (sections Background, Edward I steps in, Claimants, Claims, Election; secondary)

## double-election-and-battle-1314
- Mechanism: a body of electors splits and chooses two kings; settled by battle, then a negotiated sharing of the office.
- Period and place: Holy Roman Empire (Germany), 1314–1330.
- Who may contest: the electors; the Holy Roman Empire's prince-electors were seven from the 13th century (Mainz, Cologne, Trier, Bohemia, Palatinate, Saxony, Brandenburg) and the Golden Bull of 1356 became the fundamental law of elections. In 1314 four electors chose Frederick the Fair at Sachsenhausen on 19 October and five chose Louis of Bavaria at Frankfurt the next day.
- How it is settled: rival coronations on 25 November 1314 (Louis at Aachen, Frederick at Bonn); Louis won the Battle of Mühldorf on 28 September 1322 and held Frederick about three years; on 13 March 1325 Frederick renounced the crown and was released; a dual kingship followed in the Treaty of Ulm (7 January 1326); Louis undermined it in February 1327. The pope (John XXII) opposed Louis from 1316, and declared the dual-kingship treaty invalid.
- Observed rates or durations: about eight years from double election to battle, 11 years to Frederick's death on 13 January 1330. The papal see was vacant about two years from April 1314.
- When no one prevails: joint rule (dual kingship) was tried and lasted only a short time per the article; the majority rule of the Golden Bull is not described in the pages fetched.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_IV,_Holy_Roman_Emperor (sections Election as German King and conflict with Frederick the Fair, Dual Kingship); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_election (sections Prince-electors, Background; secondary)

## regency-during-a-minority-henry-iii
- Mechanism: a child heir succeeds amid civil war; a regent, appointed by the leading lords and the papal legate, rules until the minority ends.
- Period and place: England, 1216–1227.
- Who may contest: not stated in the source for regency in general (the Regency article says only that regency in France was traditionally for the princes of the blood). In 1216 the rival claimant was Prince Louis of France, who held part of England in the First Barons' War.
- How it is settled: Henry was nine at John's death in October 1216; John had asked that William Marshal have guardianship; Marshal accepted only after Cardinal Guala and Earl Ranulf de Blondeville asked him; Henry was crowned at Gloucester on 28 October 1216 under the legate; Peter des Roches was made guardian so Marshal could lead the war; loyalists won at Lincoln and Sandwich in 1217; the Treaty of Lambeth (September 1217) paid Louis about £6,700 to leave; Hubert de Burgh succeeded Marshal as regent in 1219; Henry took formal control in January 1227.
- Observed rates or durations: 10 years from accession to formal control; some contemporaries held he stayed a legal minor until his 21st birthday in 1228. Regency 1216–1219 under Marshal.
- When no one prevails: not stated in the source. A general dispute process for regencies is also not given; the Regency article's only example is the 1560 French deal in which Antoine of Navarre gave up his regency claim for his brother Condé's release and the Privy Council made Catherine de' Medici governor.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_III_of_England (section Accession and minority; secondary; first ~100,000 characters read); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regency (sections Examples, Europe; secondary)

## usurper-later-recognised
- Mechanism: a claimant deposes the holder by force and is recognised by the nobility, then by the next generation's settlement or marriage.
- Period and place: England, 1399–1487.
- Who may contest: Henry Bolingbroke (1399, male-line argument), the House of York (the Act of Accord is called controversial; its date and content are not stated in the page read), Henry Tudor (1485, claim through his mother Margaret Beaufort).
- How it is settled: Bolingbroke invaded in June 1399 with a force that grew quickly, deposed Richard II and was crowned Henry IV, recognised by much of the disaffected nobility; Yorkists won at Towton and Edward IV was crowned in June 1461; Henry Tudor defeated and killed Richard III at Bosworth in 1485 and married Elizabeth of York, Edward IV's eldest daughter, uniting the houses (marriage date not stated); remaining Yorkist opposition was defeated at Stoke Field in 1487.
- Observed rates or durations: not stated in the source for rates; the Lancastrian male line ended in 1471 per the article.
- When no one prevails: not stated in the source.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_the_Roses (sections Deposition by Henry Bolingbroke (1399), Claims of the two Houses, Yorkist triumph (1461), Defeat of Richard III; secondary; first ~100,000 characters read)

## aggregate-rate-by-rule-type
- Mechanism: not a contest mechanism; the only quantitative evidence found on how often succession rules fail.
- Period and place: 42 European states, 1000–1800, 961 monarchs.
- Who may contest: not stated in the source.
- How it is settled: not stated in the source.
- Observed rates or durations: states using primogeniture saw fewer monarchs deposed than states using other succession rules; no figure per rule is given in the abstract. The authors link primogeniture to state strength: by 1801 every European monarchy had adopted it or been defeated by foreign enemies. Goody 1966: no rate found; searched: library catalogue records (British Museum, Holy Cross) and web search for Goody's rates by rule type; nothing found, text not opened. Pollock & Maitland and Stubbs give no per-rule rate either.
- When no one prevails: not stated in the source.
- Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/apsrev/v108y2014i02p438-453_00.html (Kokkonen & Sundell 2014, *APSR* 108(2) pp. 438–453, abstract only)
