## manorial-court-baron-action-wager-of-law
- Period: sample rolls of 1246–1248 (Abbey of Bec manors); the same custom is described for the 13th century generally. Applying it to c. 1300 is an inference from these earlier rolls, not a source statement.
- Court and presiding officer: the lord's manorial court (court baron) at a manor such as Tooting (Surrey) or Wantage (Berkshire); the rolls in these entries name no judge. Maitland argues the steward toured with the rolls but that judgment was made by the suitors, citing the maxim that the court, not the lord, gives judgment.
- Who judges: the suitors of the court (the tenants who owe suit of court), per Maitland's Introduction; the steward is not named in these entries.
- How a case begins: a private plaint. The plaintiff sues another tenant (e.g. for crop damage by animals, or a 16d debt) and produces suit, meaning supporting witnesses, with the defendant under pledges.
- Proof: if the defendant denies the claim wholesale he is sent "to his law" and makes it with oath-helpers (the Tooting entry says five compurgators, "sixth hand"; the number for other cases is not stated in the pages read). No jury is used in these entries. In the king's court c. 1300 the general rule in debt was that, without a sealed document, the defendant could wage law (Pollock & Maitland, vol. 2, pp. 212–213); the number of oath-helpers in royal practice is disputed in P&M's notes (25 or 50 mentioned) and is not used here.
- Appeal or removal: no removal to the king's court appears in these entries; not stated in the source. Maitland (Introduction, about p. xvii) notes only that a writ of right had to be brought in the lord's own court.
- Source: Maitland 1889, Select Pleas in Manorial and Other Seignorial Courts, vol. 1, Introduction (about pp. xi–xli) and Bec rolls 1246–1248 (Tooting, Wantage), https://archive.org/stream/selectpleasinman00mait/selectpleasinman00mait_djvu.txt ; Pollock & Maitland, History of English Law vol. 2, pp. 212–214, https://archive.org/stream/historyofenglish02poll_0/historyofenglish02poll_0_djvu.txt

## manorial-court-jury-inquest-on-right-to-land
- Period: 1248 (Ruislip, Middlesex, Bec rolls); c. 1300 use is an inference.
- Court and presiding officer: the lord's manorial court; presiding officer not named in the entry.
- Who judges: twelve sworn jurors find the fact; the court (suitors) then gives the result, in the entry seisin follows the finding.
- How a case begins: a litigant pays the lord a fee (12d in the entry) for a jury to decide which of two parties has the better right to a piece of land (a headland). The other party may object, here that she held only as guardian of a minor heir and so could not bring the land into judgment.
- Proof: inquest by twelve sworn jurors; their finding decides who has the greater right and receives seisin. The entry is partly cut off in the text read.
- Appeal or removal: not stated in the source.
- Source: Maitland 1889, Select Pleas, vol. 1, Ruislip roll 1248, https://archive.org/stream/selectpleasinman00mait/selectpleasinman00mait_djvu.txt

## manorial-court-presentment-by-jurors-or-homage
- Period: Maitland's Introduction covers 13th-century leets; the explanation of the court baron's homage is from a modern archive glossary and a general encyclopaedia and is not a c. 1300 source.
- Court and presiding officer: the lord's steward presides in the later private leet (Maitland, about p. xxviii); for the court baron, the lord or steward (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorial_court , a general summary, not a primary or period source).
- Who judges: a jury of twelve, often the chief pledges, makes the presentment before the steward (Maitland, about pp. xxxiv–xxxv). The homage is the body of tenants who attend the manor court; the Nottingham glossary defines a presentment as the jury's statement of matters for the court to deal with.
- How a case begins: the jury, or individual tithingmen, present offences to the court without a private plaintiff; Maitland ties this to frankpledge and to the sheriff's tourn, where chief pledges answered the articles of the view and a twelve-man freeholder jury made the final presentment (about pp. xxviii–xxx).
- Proof: the jury's own presentment stands as the proof. Per Maitland, presentments by a jury of twelve on matters not touching freehold were afterwards untraversable, while those touching freehold could be traversed (about p. xxviii); whether this held c. 1300 in every court is not stated.
- Appeal or removal: not stated in the source for presentments. Maitland notes only that some bodies (Templars, Hospitallers) could be sued solely in the king's court (about p. xxv) and that lords' men were exempt from suit at hundred and county courts (about p. xxiv).
- Source: Maitland 1889, Select Pleas, vol. 1, Introduction, https://archive.org/stream/selectpleasinman00mait/selectpleasinman00mait_djvu.txt ; University of Nottingham Manuscripts and Special Collections, manorial glossary (homage, presentment, court baron, suit of court), https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/manuscriptsandspecialcollections/researchguidance/manorial/glossary.aspx ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorial_court
