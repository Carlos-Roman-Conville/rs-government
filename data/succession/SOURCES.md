## english-descent-with-representation
- Rule: Land descends to the issue of the last holder, males before females, the eldest male alone among males of equal degree; a dead person's descendants stand in that person's place (representation), so a dead eldest son's children take before his younger brothers.
- Period and place: England, as stated by Blackstone (published 1766); the period the rules cover is not stated in the chapter text fetched.
- Who is eligible: the issue of the person last seised (holding the land), without limit of degree, through males and females; with no sons, the daughters together.
- How the successor is chosen: by canons (Blackstone's numbered rules 1–4): descent goes down and never up; sons before daughters; the eldest son alone, and if there are no sons, all daughters together as coparceners; the descendants of a dead heir take that heir's place, by family branch (per stirpes) and not by head (per capita), indefinitely.
- When no one qualifies: if there is no heir, the land escheats to the lord; it does not go to a grandparent.
- Source: Blackstone, *Commentaries* book II ch. 14 ("Of Title by Descent"), canons 1–4, https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/blackstone_bk2ch14.asp
- Note: Pollock & Maitland vol. II "Inheritance" (pp. 252–381 per the contents listing at https://muse.jhu.edu/book/18251) is the medieval treatment and should be checked for when representation was settled; not read here.

## english-collateral-heirs
- Rule: When the last holder leaves no issue, the inheritance goes to collateral kin (brothers, uncles, cousins), by the rest of the canons of descent.
- Period and place: England, as stated by Blackstone (published 1766); other dates not stated in the source.
- Who is eligible: first, the blood of the first purchaser (the person who first acquired the estate other than by descent); among collaterals, the nearest kinsman of the whole blood (related through both parents); half-blood kin are excluded while any whole-blood kinsman exists, even a more distant one; in the collateral line the male (paternal) line is preferred to the female, except where the land actually came down from a female ancestor, when the other line is favoured.
- How the successor is chosen: canons 5–7: look to the first purchaser's blood; take the nearest of the whole blood, with representation applying (a nephew takes through his dead parent); prefer the paternal line over the maternal, subject to the exception above. The first three canons still govern within the chosen line.
- When no one qualifies: the estate escheats to the lord.
- Source: Blackstone, *Commentaries* book II ch. 14, canons 5–7 and the discussion of escheat, https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/blackstone_bk2ch14.asp
- Note: the page summary counted seven canons; the chapter's discussion of purchase and blood-line is not numbered further.

## tanistry
- Rule: A tanist (heir) is elected from the ruler's kin while the ruler is still alive, and becomes ruler on the death; a new tanist is then elected.
- Period and place: Ireland, the Isle of Man and parts of Scotland; the page gives the 4th century AD for Ireland but notes a footnote saying the evidence points to about a century after the Anglo-Norman invasion, so its dates conflict. It ran through the Middle Ages, was replaced by primogeniture as English- and Scots-speaking rulers took Gaelic lands, and persisted among Gaelic local chiefs until the 17th century. In Scotland a legal decision under James VI ended it (the case is named on the page only as Davis 1608, *Case of Tanistry*).
- Who is eligible: adult males of the ruler's kin group, commonly the derbfine (descended in the male line from a common great-grandfather), often narrowed to the gelfhine (from a common grandfather); in Scotland descent through the female line was also accepted. The ruler had to be of full age, in command of his faculties and without a serious physical or mental blemish. Clients who were not kin of the ruling line were excluded.
- How the successor is chosen: the eligible kinsmen are the electorate and also the candidates; the election is held in full assembly in advance of any vacancy, so the heir is known.
- When no one qualifies: not stated in the source.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanistry (secondary; it cites MacNeill 1934, *Early Irish Laws and Institutions*, Warntjes 2004 in *Journal of Medieval History*, Jaski 1995, Byrne 1973 *Irish Kings and High Kings*, and Davis 1608 *Case of Tanistry*, none opened)

## rus-rota-rotation
- Rule: The grand-princely throne passes sideways among brothers, then to the eldest son of the eldest brother who reigned; each death moves every prince up the ladder of principalities.
- Period and place: Kievan Rus' from the 11th century (credited to Yaroslav the Wise, d. 1054, who assigned his sons principalities by seniority), then appanages and early Moscow; after the 1097 Liubech conference some lands were granted as patrimonial lands outside the rota. Traces are claimed in the Muscovite War of Succession, 1425–1453. The page calls it a historiographical concept and records that critics find the succession chaotic.
- Who is eligible: only princes whose fathers had held the throne; sons of a man who died before reaching the throne became *izgoi* and they and their descendants could not rule.
- How the successor is chosen: on the grand prince's death the next most senior prince moves to Kiev and the others shift up to the next principality on the ladder. Seniority was by birth in the 11th century; by the mid-12th it depended on military control of Kiev (Oleksiy Tolochko, as cited on the page). Usual lateral run: up to the fourth brother, then the eldest son of the eldest reigning brother (the page's wording is "usually").
- When no one qualifies: not stated in the source; only the izgoi exclusion is given. Janet Martin is cited as suggesting that warfare was a breach of the order, not its normal working.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rota_system (secondary; it cites Martin 1995 *Medieval Russia, 980–1584*, Kollmann 1990 "Collateral Succession in Kievan Rus'", Ostrowski 2012 and 2018, none opened)

## buganda-designation-by-the-king
- Rule: The reigning Kabaka (king) picks one prince as successor with a special council's advice, and keeps the name secret until his death.
- Period and place: the Kingdom of Buganda; the period is not stated in the page section read. The 1888 exception it names is the only dated event.
- Who is eligible: all princes are treated alike before a coronation; Buganda has no crown prince; by tradition the firstborn prince (Kiweewa) may not become king, with 1888 the one exception given.
- How the successor is chosen: a special council studies the young princes' behaviour and character and recommends; the king selects one; in a secret ceremony the head of the council gives the chosen prince a piece of bark cloth; at the late king's lying in state the chosen prince lays it on the body and so reveals himself. The spiritual king (Juma Katebe) takes part in the rites that crown the new king.
- When no one qualifies: not stated in the source.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabaka_of_Buganda, section "Election of kings" (secondary). The scholarly source for Buganda in the Goody volume is Southwold, "Succession to the Throne in Buganda", in Goody (ed.) 1966, not opened.
- Note: the same page lists rapid changes of king in 1888–1889 during a religious civil war and does not explain them.
