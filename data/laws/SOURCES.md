## merton-1235-c4-approvement-of-commons
- Source: Statute of Merton 1235 (20 Hen. III) c.4, listed as "Commons Act 1236"; summaries at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Merton and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commons_Act_1236
- Who made it: the king with the magnates in council (Henry III), as a statute.
- Applies to: England; a lord of a manor against his freehold tenants who had common of pasture on waste, wood or pasture.
- Act regulated: the lord enclosing ("approving") part of the common land to himself.
- Modality: permit
- Condition: enough pasture is left for the tenants' holdings.
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: the enclosure stands; if too little is left the tenants may complain by writ (the writ and its remedy are not stated in the source checked).
- Enforcer and procedure: the king's courts on a tenant's writ; the procedure is not stated in the source.
- Period in force: 1235 until repeal in England and Wales by the Statute Law Revision Act 1953 (2 & 3 Eliz. 2 c. 5), in force 18 December 1953 (Northern Ireland: Statute Law Revision Act 1950); per https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commons_Act_1236 , which links the repealing Acts at https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Eliz2/2-3/5/ and https://legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo6/14/6/ . The legislation.gov.uk record for the chapter itself could not be fetched (404 on the URLs tried), so the date follows the Wikipedia list; the earlier "Commons Act 2006" was not found in any source.
- Note: first English statutory enclosure power. The precise writ is not given in the pages checked.

## westminster-2-1285-c1-de-donis
- Source: Statute of Westminster II 1285 (13 Edw. I) c.1 *De donis conditionalibus*, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/aep/Edw1/13/1
- Who made it: the king (Edward I) in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: England; land given to a man and the heirs of his body (or on a similar condition), and the people who hold it.
- Act regulated: alienating (selling or fining away) land held under such a conditional gift.
- Modality: prohibit
- Condition: the land was given with a condition in the deed, and the gift was made after the statute.
- Exceptions: gifts made before the statute are not affected.
- Consequence: the sale or fine (a court-recorded transfer) is void; the land passes to the issue, or reverts to the giver or the giver's heirs if the line fails.
- Enforcer and procedure: the king's courts; heirs with a reversion who are of full age, in England and free need not make a claim.
- Period in force: 1285 onward; end date not stated in the source.
- Note: a widow's second husband takes nothing of the entailed land, and the children of that marriage cannot inherit it.

## westminster-2-1285-c46-approvement
- Source: Statute of Westminster II 1285 (13 Edw. I) c.46, "Commons Act 1285", listed at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/aep/Edw1/13/46/data.html and described at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commons_Act_1285 (secondary summaries only; the chapter text was not fetched).
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: England; lords and neighbours who share common pasture.
- Act regulated: a lord approving (enclosing) common against neighbouring commoners who are not his own tenants.
- Modality: permit
- Condition: sufficient common is left to the neighbours.
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: not stated in the source.
- Enforcer and procedure: the king's courts; the writ is not stated in the source.
- Period in force: 1285 until repeal by the Commons Act 2006.
- Note: extends the Merton rule from the lord's own tenants to neighbouring commoners (per the summaries).

## winchester-1285-hue-and-cry
- Source: Statute of Winchester 1285 (13 Edw. I), https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Statute_of_Winchester (summary of its clauses on pursuit and the hundred's liability)
- Who made it: the king (Edward I) in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: all England; every town, hundred and county, with sheriffs and bailiffs.
- Act regulated: pursuing a felon or robber.
- Modality: require
- Condition: a felony or robbery has been done.
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: the hundred, with its franchises, answers for robberies within it; the county has 40 days to settle the loss or produce the offenders.
- Enforcer and procedure: watchmen raise the hue and cry and pursue with their town and neighbouring towns; sheriffs and bailiffs with bailiwicks join with the county as far as horses and armour allow.
- Period in force: 1285 until repealed in the nineteenth century (date not stated in the source).
- Note: collective liability moves a loss from the victim to the hundred.

## winchester-1285-watch-and-ward
- Source: Statute of Winchester 1285 (13 Edw. I), https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Statute_of_Winchester
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: walled towns, boroughs and other towns; strangers and lodgers.
- Act regulated: keeping the night watch, closing gates, and holding strangers.
- Modality: require
- Condition: from sunset to sunrise, between Ascension Day and Michaelmas.
- Exceptions: strangers with no ground for suspicion are let go in the morning.
- Consequence: a host answers for his lodgers; no one is punished for detaining a stranger in this way.
- Enforcer and procedure: watchmen (six men per gate in cities, twelve in boroughs, six or four in towns by size) hold strangers until morning; suspects go to the sheriff; bailiffs inquire into lodgers weekly or every fifteen days.
- Period in force: 1285 until repealed in the nineteenth century (date not stated in the source).
- Note: a walled-town rule; whether an unwalled village is covered is not stated in the source.

## winchester-1285-arms-assize
- Source: Statute of Winchester 1285 (13 Edw. I), arms clause, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Statute_of_Winchester
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: men aged 15 to 60, by wealth.
- Act regulated: keeping arms.
- Modality: require
- Condition: the man's land or goods; the richest keep a hauberk, iron helm, lance, knife and horse; men with under 40 shillings of yearly land keep falchions, glaives, knives or similar small arms.
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: defaults are reported to the justices; the penalty is not stated in the source checked.
- Enforcer and procedure: two constables per hundred and franchise view the arms twice a year and report defaults to visiting justices, who report to the king at parliament.
- Period in force: 1285 onward; end date not stated in the source.
- Note: the middle wealth bands are not in the summary fetched.

## winchester-1285-highways
- Source: Statute of Winchester 1285 (13 Edw. I), highways clause, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Statute_of_Winchester
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: lords and landholders beside roads between market towns.
- Act regulated: leaving cover beside the road.
- Modality: require
- Condition: the road runs between market towns; woods, hedges and ditches within 200 feet of each side are cleared.
- Exceptions: large trees are allowed if the ground beneath is clear.
- Consequence: a lord whose land is not cleared is liable for a robbery there; for a murder he pays a fine set at the king's discretion.
- Enforcer and procedure: the county helps if the lord cannot clear the land alone.
- Period in force: 1285 onward; end date not stated in the source.
- Note: parks beside highways must be cut back to a 200-foot border or enclosed by a wall, ditch or hedge.

## mortmain-1279-de-viris-religiosis
- Source: Statute of Mortmain 1279 (7 Edw. I) *De viris religiosis*, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Mortmain
- Who made it: the king (Edward I) in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: religious persons and houses; land held of lords in England.
- Act regulated: acquiring land by any means, even by buying it for full rent.
- Modality: prohibit
- Condition: the land comes into the hands of a religious house or person.
- Exceptions: a lord who does not act in time lets the gift stand; a licence exception is not described in the source (the page notes licences were easy to get under Henry III, before the statute).
- Consequence: the land is forfeit; the immediate lord has a short time to enter, then the next lord up the chain, up to the king.
- Enforcer and procedure: the chain of overlords by entry within fixed deadlines (their length is not stated in the source).
- Period in force: 1279 onward; end date not stated in the source.
- Note: a lord's inaction could let land pass to the Church.

## quia-emptores-1290
- Source: Quia Emptores 1290 (18 Edw. I) c.1, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/aep/Edw1/18/1
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: freeholders holding in fee simple.
- Act regulated: selling land.
- Modality: permit
- Condition: the seller holds in fee simple; the statute takes effect from St Andrew's Day the following year.
- Exceptions: the page checked names no exemptions other than the fee simple limit.
- Consequence: the buyer holds directly of the seller's chief lord, on the same services (in proportion if only part is sold); no new middle lordship is made.
- Enforcer and procedure: the chief lord keeps his escheats, marriages and wardships; the procedure is not stated in the source.
- Period in force: 1290 until repeal; the date is not stated in the source.
- Note: its mortmain section is marked as repealed in the version fetched.

## assize-of-bread-and-ale-c1266
- Source: Assize of Bread and Ale (13th century; placed c. 1266), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assize_of_Bread_and_Ale ; secondary works cited there include Davis 2004 "Baking for the common good" and Bennett 2004 *Women in the Medieval English Countryside*.
- Who made it: the king in parliament; earlier forms go back to Henry II and John (the article's account).
- Applies to: bakers and brewers selling in England, in towns and on manors.
- Act regulated: selling bread and ale.
- Modality: require
- Condition: bread keeps a fixed price and the loaf weight varies with the price of wheat (one weight at 12 pence a quarter, falling by steps for each sixpence added up to 20 shillings a quarter); ale is priced against wheat, barley and oats, with town brewers selling more gallons per penny than country ones.
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: amercement (a fine); the amount is not stated in the source. The pillory and tumbrel are not mentioned on the page checked.
- Enforcer and procedure: in the country, the lord at his court (three-weekly); ale-tasters chosen yearly at the court leet and sworn to assay the ale.
- Period in force: 13th century until repeal in 1863.
- Note: the exact loaf weights are not on the page checked.

## forest-charter-1217-venison
- Source: Charter of the Forest 1217 clause 10, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_of_the_Forest
- Who made it: the regency government for Henry III (issued 6 November 1217), as a charter; reissued 1225.
- Applies to: all persons within the royal forest.
- Act regulated: taking the king's venison (deer).
- Modality: prohibit
- Condition: the taking is in the forest without leave.
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: no one loses life or limb for it; the page says offenders could still be fined or imprisoned (amounts not stated in the source).
- Enforcer and procedure: verderers' courts, per the page (no clause number given).
- Period in force: 1217 onward; end date not stated in the source.
- Note: replaces the earlier forest-law mutilations. Vert (the greenery deer feed on) has no clause number on the page checked.

## forest-charter-1217-common-and-agistment
- Source: Charter of the Forest 1217 clauses 1 and 9, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_of_the_Forest
- Who made it: the regency government for Henry III, as a charter.
- Applies to: persons accustomed to common in the forest; every man with woods inside it.
- Act regulated: using common pasture and agisting (grazing) one's own wood.
- Modality: permit
- Condition: the person is accustomed to the common (clause 1); the wood is his own (clause 9).
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: the use is lawful; the penalty for interference is not stated in the source.
- Enforcer and procedure: verderers' courts, per the page.
- Period in force: 1217 onward; end date not stated in the source.
- Note: pannage and estovers are named on the page as rights of the period but are not tied to a clause there.

## ordinance-of-labourers-1349-service
- Source: Ordinance of Labourers 1349 (23 Edw. III), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinance_of_Labourers_1349
- Who made it: the king (Edward III) in council, by ordinance, 18 June 1349.
- Applies to: able-bodied people under 60 with no land or means of their own; employers.
- Act regulated: working when required, and the wages paid.
- Modality: require
- Condition: the person has no independent means and is asked to serve; wages are held to pre-plague rates (about 1346–47).
- Exceptions: those with their own land or means.
- Consequence: leaving service without reasonable cause means imprisonment, and so does hiring such a worker; paying or taking more than the rate means repaying double; a lord who breaks it pays treble damages.
- Enforcer and procedure: sheriffs, constables, mayors and bailiffs arrest; offences proved by two true men; to be proclaimed publicly.
- Period in force: 1349, then reinforced by the 1351 statute.
- Note: negligent mayors and bailiffs also face treble damages and punishment.

## ordinance-of-labourers-1349-almsgiving
- Source: Ordinance of Labourers 1349, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinance_of_Labourers_1349

## statute-of-labourers-1351-wages
- Source: Statute of Labourers 1351 (25 Edw. III Stat. 2), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Labourers_1351 (the Fordham text at sourcebooks.fordham.edu returned a server error and was not read)
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: servants, labourers and artificers in England, including London and boroughs, inside and outside franchises.
- Act regulated: paying or taking wages above the set rates.
- Modality: prohibit
- Condition: rates pegged to 1347 or a few years earlier: haymaking up to 1d a day; mowing 5d an acre or 5d a day; reaping 2d in the first week of August and 3d in the second; threshing up to 2½d a quarter of wheat or rye and 1½d for barley, beans, peas and oats; master carpenter 3d, other carpenter 2d; master mason 4d, other mason 3d, mason's servant 1½d; tiler 3d, boy 1½d; ploughmen and shepherds hired by the year.
- Exceptions: regions where pay had been lower keep the lower rate.
- Consequence: fine, ransom and imprisonment at the justices' discretion; money taken by officials goes to the tax collectors; overpayment may be recovered by suit.
- Enforcer and procedure: justices sitting at least four times a year in each county; stewards, bailiffs and constables are sworn to report.
- Period in force: 1351 onward; end date not stated in the source.
- Note: the page says enforcement was weak and aimed at labourers more than employers.

## statute-of-labourers-1351-oath-and-stocks
- Source: Statute of Labourers 1351, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Labourers_1351
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: workers sworn to keep the rates; able-bodied persons without means.
- Act regulated: refusing the oath, breaking it, or refusing to serve.
- Modality: require
- Condition: the worker has been called to take the oath, or has no means and is able to work.
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: breaking the oath means 40 days in prison, three months on a repeat conviction, and double for each later offence; refusing the oath means three days or more in the stocks, or gaol; refusing to serve means gaol until the worker finds a guarantor.
- Enforcer and procedure: stocks to be built in every town before Pentecost; fugitive workers may be seized and held in the county's chief gaol.
- Period in force: 1351 onward; end date not stated in the source.
- Note: the page does not mention branding.

## treason-1351
- Source: Treason Act 1351 (25 Edw. III Stat. 5 c.2), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treason_Act_1351
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: all subjects.
- Act regulated: compassing the death of the king, queen or heir; violating the queen, the eldest unmarried daughter or the heir's wife; levying war; adhering to the king's enemies; counterfeiting the great or privy seal or the coin; killing the chancellor, treasurer or certain judges in office.
- Modality: prohibit
- Condition: an overt act is proved (the page reports this as the courts' reading).
- Exceptions: adhering to rebels or pirates is not adhering to enemies (the courts' reading, per the page).
- Consequence: high treason: hanging, drawing and quartering for men, drawing and burning for women, property to the Crown; petty treason (a servant killing a master, a wife a husband): drawing and hanging, property to the immediate lord.
- Enforcer and procedure: the king's judges; a doubtful case not listed waits for parliament and the king to decide whether it is treason or felony.
- Period in force: 1351 onward, with penalty changes in 1828, 1870 and 1998 (per the page).
- Note: petty treason was abolished in 1828.

## diet-and-apparel-1363-yeomen-and-servants
- Source: Statute Concerning Diet and Apparel 1363 (37 Edw. III cc.1, 3–19), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_Concerning_Diet_and_Apparel_1363 ; class limits come from a search summary only (https://rosaliegilbert.com/sumptuarylaws.html); the statute text itself was not read.
- Who made it: the king in parliament, as a statute.
- Applies to: yeomen, servants, ploughmen and carters, and their families.
- Act regulated: wearing cloth, furs and ornaments above one's rank.
- Modality: prohibit
- Condition: per the summary: yeomen, whole-garment cloth up to 40 shillings, no jewels, gold, silver, embroidery or silk; servants of lords, about two marks; land workers with under 40 shillings in goods, coarse cloth only (blanket and russet at 12d an ell) and rope-type linen belts.
- Exceptions: some small furs (lamb, rabbit, cat, fox) are noted for yeomen in the summary.
- Consequence: not stated in the source.
- Enforcer and procedure: not stated in the source.
- Period in force: 1363; the Wikipedia page gives repeal for England in 1863 and Ireland in 1872. One timeline claims a repeal in 1364, which I could not confirm.
- Note: the stated purpose is to stop lower classes dressing like the elite after the plague. Low confidence on the class limits.

## frankpledge-tithing-duty
- Source: view of frankpledge and tithing, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankpledge (a secondary summary; the Magna Carta clause on the sheriff's view was not confirmed on that page).
- Who made it: custom, with royal confirmation; a lord or borough may hold the view as a franchise.
- Applies to: males over 12 enrolled in a tithing of about ten under a chief-pledge or tithingman; women, clergy and richer freemen exempt.
- Act regulated: producing a tithing member who is accused.
- Modality: require
- Condition: a member is suspected of a crime and summoned.
- Exceptions: if a summoned man flees, the rest of the tithing may clear themselves on oath.
- Consequence: otherwise the tithing may be fined for his offence; the amount is not stated in the source.
- Enforcer and procedure: the sheriff's tourn conducts the view and takes a tithing penny; lords or boroughs holding the franchise do it in their leet.
- Period in force: before 1215 into the later fourteenth century, when the system declined and constables under justices of the peace took over (the page).
- Note: early on, only landholders could be made to pay.

## gleaning-bylaw
- Source: Warren O. Ault, *Open-Field Farming in Medieval England* (1972), via the secondary description at https://lahs.org.uk/blog/the-early-fifteenth-century-wymeswold-bye-laws-revisited , which says gleaning was a customary right of the poorest and that later by-laws (Wymeswold; Islip 1407) limited it, e.g. a two-pence-a-day wage test at Wymeswold. The earlier textbook excerpt URL is dead and no working source was found for the list of permitted gleaners, the fine or the c. 1286 date below; those lines are unsupported by a working source. Ault's book itself was not opened.
- Who made it: the village community, by common consent, recorded as an entry in the manorial court roll.
- Applies to: persons in the manor's open fields at harvest.
- Act regulated: gleaning, picking up fallen grain after reaping.
- Modality: permit
- Condition: the person is one of the elderly, small children, or the ill or handicapped (the textbook excerpt); others are barred.
- Exceptions: not stated in the source.
- Consequence: a fine at the court; the amount for gleaning is not stated in the source.
- Enforcer and procedure: the lord's court, with presentments by the village; the wording of any one by-law is not stated in the source checked.
- Period in force: c. 1286 (the Broughton rolls cited) through the later Middle Ages.
- Note: the sheaves by-law below shows what fines looked like at the same kind of court.

## forestalling-regrating
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestalling (it says the Statute of Forestallers was probably never passed by formal process, and that Domesday lists "foresteel" under Edward the Confessor).
- Who made it: custom; later rules issued by Marshalsea officers under Edward I are called the Statute of Forestallers.
- Applies to: traders in markets and fairs.
- Act regulated: forestalling (buying before goods reach market, or dissuading sellers) and regrating (buying grain or dry provisions in a market and reselling in it or within four miles).
- Modality: prohibit
- Condition: the buying or discouraging is done to raise the price.
- Exceptions: licensed traders (a sixteenth-century rule; none stated for the medieval period).
- Consequence: a fine; repeat offenders may be pilloried; amounts not stated in the source.
- Enforcer and procedure: not stated in the source.
- Period in force: recognised as an offence by 1321; abolished 1844.
- Note: forestalling was often a catch-all for marketing offences.
